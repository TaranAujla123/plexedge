02 — The Idea

They call it “house-hacking.” We grew up calling it home.

A — The Fear

The idea is old and simple: own a small multi-unit building, live in the best part of it, and let the other homes help carry the mortgage. Across Ontario it gets branded “house-hacking” — a clever trick, trailed by every nightmare-landlord story you’ve heard. That word is what scares good people away from a genuinely good home.

B — The Reframe

In Montreal, we never used it. For generations it was simply how families lived — the owner’s side the largest and the nicest, with the yard, the garage and the bedrooms together, and steady neighbours in the homes alongside. Done properly, it isn’t a hack. It’s live-in ownership: a real home that quietly pays for itself.

C — The Craft

The building was never the hard part. The craft is living well alongside your tenants — the courtesy, the boundaries, the small rules that keep everyone comfortable. That is what we grew up with, and the part most owners here were simply never taught.

Since 2000, I helped run my family’s multiplexes in Montreal alongside my father and brother — while I was still studying and working. It was never a plan or a title. It was simply how we lived, and I don’t think any of us would have called ourselves “landlords.” The buildings just worked: the rents covered the mortgage, so we lived in our own home without really paying for it. That is the quiet math of a well-run plex, and it is the first thing real estate ever taught me.

I went on to invest on my own before moving to the deal side as a licensed Salesperson. I have lived in a plex, collected the rent and fixed what broke — so I read these buildings as homes and businesses, not just listings.

We design for what a multiplex is — not for show.

A multiplex is income-producing housing. The money belongs in the things people actually live with: layout, durability, soundproofing, insulation, mechanical systems, good natural light and proper separation between homes. That is where it should go.

Expensive finishes only make sense when the rent supports them. We underwrite the building first, then design accordingly — cost-disciplined, not stripped-down — so the money goes where it earns its keep, not into flourishes tenants won’t pay for.
